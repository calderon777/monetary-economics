Debate Lab: Classicals vs Keynesians

This ludic activity turns Topics 1-3 into a live debate. You will prompt two AI “teams” that represent rival schools of thought. Your job is to test their claims with your own questions.

Debate Cast

Classicals - Leon Walras: General equilibrium, Walras’ Law, classical dichotomy, long-run neutrality. - Alfred Marshall: Cambridge cash-balance view, money demand and velocity, money as a convenience asset.

Keynesians - John Maynard Keynes: Liquidity preference, money demand motives, liquidity trap. - John Hicks: IS-LM synthesis, short-run disequilibrium and policy transmission.

How to Use This Debate

  1. Read the short scripted opening below.
  2. Choose a question from the prompt bank or write your own.
  3. Run three rounds (Round 1, Round 2, Round 3).
  4. Ask the Classicals to respond, then the Keynesians.
  5. Follow up with a challenge question.

Scripted Opening (Read Aloud)

Moderator: Today we debate whether money is neutral and how monetary economies adjust. Classicals, your opening statement.

Walras (Classical): In general equilibrium, relative prices adjust so all markets clear. Money is a unit of account and medium of exchange, but the real allocation is governed by preferences, technology, and endowments. Changes in money scale nominal prices, not real outcomes.

Marshall (Classical): The Cambridge view emphasizes money demand as a stable fraction of income. If money supply changes, the price level adjusts to restore desired cash balances. In the long run, inflation is a monetary phenomenon.

Moderator: Keynesians, your opening statement.

Keynes (Keynesian): In a monetary economy, people hold money for transactions, precaution, and speculation. When confidence collapses, they hoard liquidity. Then Walras’ Law no longer implies Say’s Law; demand can fall short and unemployment can persist.

Hicks (Keynesian): The IS-LM framework shows interaction between goods and money markets. When interest rates are near the floor, the LM curve becomes flat and conventional monetary policy loses traction. Short-run real effects are unavoidable.

Three-Round Debate Script (Read Aloud)

Round 1: Neutrality

Moderator: Round 1 asks whether money is neutral in the long run and what that implies for real outcomes.

Walras (Classical): Long-run equilibrium depends on real factors. Monetary changes scale all prices without altering real allocations.

Keynes (Keynesian): Even if long-run neutrality holds, the path is not neutral. Short-run demand failures can lock in unemployment.

Round 2: Liquidity Preference

Moderator: Round 2 asks whether money demand is stable or interest-sensitive.

Marshall (Classical): The cash-balance ratio is stable enough to anchor the price level. Velocity shifts, but not capriciously.

Hicks (Keynesian): When rates are low, speculative demand dominates. The LM curve flattens and money demand becomes highly elastic.

Round 3: Policy at Low Rates

Moderator: Round 3 asks what policy can do when rates approach the floor.

Walras (Classical): Structural adjustment and price flexibility restore equilibrium. Policy should avoid distorting relative prices.

Keynes (Keynesian): Fiscal expansion and unconventional policy are needed when liquidity traps block normal adjustment.

Prompt Bank (Choose One)

  1. If money is just a veil, why did output and employment collapse during the Great Depression?
  2. Does Walras’ Law imply Say’s Law in a monetary economy? Explain why or why not.
  3. How does the Cambridge cash-balance equation differ from the quantity theory in practice?
  4. What makes the money market “exceptional” compared to other markets?
  5. Is the liquidity trap a theoretical curiosity or a real policy problem?
  6. Can monetary policy be effective when interest rates are near zero?
  7. Which view better explains post-2008 and post-2020 monetary conditions?
  8. Is money demand stable or interest-sensitive? Defend your answer.

Student Challenge Ideas

  • Ask each side to name one weakness in its own framework.
  • Ask the Classicals to explain unemployment without money illusion.
  • Ask the Keynesians to explain long-run inflation without money growth.
  • Ask both sides to predict what happens if money supply doubles overnight.

Reflection Questions

  1. Which side gave more convincing answers and why?
  2. Where did each side rely on assumptions that might fail in reality?
  3. How would a modern central bank combine insights from both views?
  4. What evidence would change your mind about neutrality or liquidity traps?
