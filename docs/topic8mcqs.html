Topic 8 - MCQs Formative Assessment

Author

by Dr Camilo Calderon

How to use this quiz

This quiz is designed as a formative self-assessment tool, not as a test.

Its purpose is to help you: - check your understanding of the key concepts from Topic 8, - identify which types of thinking you find more difficult, and - guide your revision before the tutorial and future assessments.

What this quiz measures

Each question belongs to one of four categories:

  • Subject Knowledge (SK): definitions, concepts, and facts.
  • Analysis (AN): understanding relationships and economic mechanisms.
  • Problem Solving (PS): applying concepts to new situations.
  • Mathematical Thinking (MT): working with equations and quantitative relationships.

Your score will be broken down by category so you can see where you are strong and where you need more practice.

How to complete the quiz

  • You may use your notes and the lecture materials.
  • Take your time — the goal is understanding, not speed.
  • Use the Hints if you get stuck.
  • You may refresh and retry the quiz as many times as you like.

How to interpret your results

  • A low score is not a problem — it tells you what to focus on.
  • If you score low in:
    • SK: revise definitions and core concepts.
    • AN: revisit the logic of the models and examples.
    • PS: practise applying concepts to new cases.
    • MT: practise the mathematical calculations and equations.

About the leaderboard

The leaderboard is optional and anonymous. It exists only to make practice more engaging.
It has no effect on your grade and is not used for evaluation.

The only purpose of this quiz is to support your learning.

Hint Think about time horizons in policymaking.
SK2) Inflation targeting involves:
Hint Transparency and numerical goals are key features.
SK3) Time inconsistency in monetary policy arises because:
Hint Consider incentives once private expectations are formed.
SK4) The inflation bias in discretionary policy occurs when:
Hint Expectations adjust, eliminating output gains but not inflation.
SK5) Credibility in monetary policy mainly affects:
Hint Expectations of future policy shape current outcomes.
SK6) A conservative central banker (Rogoff model) places:
Hint Preference parameters differ from society’s average.
SK7) Rules-based policy differs from discretion because it:
Hint Commitment changes expectations formation.
SK8) Forward guidance enhances credibility when it is:
Hint Conditional commitments reduce uncertainty.
SK9) An inflation-targeting regime with strong transparency primarily reduces:
Hint Anchored expectations dampen fluctuations.
SK10) The Barro-Gordon model predicts that under discretion, equilibrium inflation is:
Hint Policymakers have incentives to create surprise inflation.

Analysis

AN1) Why does discretionary policy generate inflation bias even when output returns to its natural level?
Hint Rational expectations eliminate systematic output gains.
AN2) If a central bank gains credibility, expected inflation becomes:
Hint Credible commitment shapes private-sector forecasts.
AN3) Why can delegation to a conservative central banker reduce inflation without large output costs?
Hint Lower expected inflation shifts the short-run Phillips curve.
AN4) If policymakers attempt surprise inflation repeatedly, what happens over time?
Hint Rational agents learn from repeated deviations.
AN5) Why does commitment technology eliminate inflation bias?
Hint Credible rules alter policymakers’ incentives.
AN6) If society places equal weight on inflation and output, but the central bank is more inflation-averse, what is the likely macro outcome?
Hint Preference parameters influence equilibrium inflation.
AN7) Why can excessive conservatism increase output variability?
Hint Stabilisation trade-offs depend on policy weights.
AN8) If a central bank announces a target but frequently misses it without explanation, credibility will:
Hint Reputation depends on consistent performance.
AN9) Why does time inconsistency arise even when policymakers are benevolent?
Hint Commitment problems stem from changing optimisation constraints.
AN10) In the Barro-Gordon framework, why is surprise inflation ineffective systematically?
Hint Anticipated policy has no real effects.

Problem Solving

PS1) A central bank promises low inflation but has a history of high inflation. What happens to expected inflation?
Hint Reputation influences expectation formation.
PS2) Suppose inflation target is 2%, but policymakers attempt to push unemployment below its natural rate. What happens in the long run?
Hint Attempts to exploit Phillips curve create inflation bias.
PS3) A newly independent central bank adopts inflation targeting and communicates clearly. What happens to inflation volatility over time?
Hint Anchored expectations stabilise inflation dynamics.
PS4) If policymakers can commit credibly, equilibrium inflation relative to discretion will be:
Hint Commitment removes the incentive for surprise inflation.
PS5) An economy experiences a negative supply shock. A highly conservative central bank reacts aggressively to inflation. What may happen to output?
Hint Strong anti-inflation response can amplify output losses.
PS6) If expected inflation falls by 2pp due to increased credibility, what happens to nominal interest rates (holding real rate constant)?
Hint Apply the Fisher equation.
PS7) Under discretion, why might inflation remain positive even if target inflation is zero?
Hint Incentives to stimulate output create bias.
PS8) If society delegates to a very conservative central banker, what trade-off arises?
Hint Preference weights affect stabilisation trade-offs.
PS9) Why does reputational loss increase the cost of breaking a commitment?
Hint Reputation affects future policy effectiveness.
PS10) If policymakers follow a strict rule that responds systematically to deviations, what happens to inflation expectations?
Hint Predictable policy improves expectation formation.
